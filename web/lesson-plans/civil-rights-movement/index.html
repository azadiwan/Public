Civil Rights Movement lesson plan

A ready-to-teach history lesson for 4th–8th grade. Every block is timed, from the hook to the exit ticket, and slides, a worksheet and an answer key are one click away.

Aligned to C3 D2.His.3.6-8 · PowerPoint, Google Slides, Canva, PDF, Word

Hook

One woman refused to give up her bus seat. How did that spark a movement?

Learning objectives

  • Analyze jim Crow laws enforced segregation in the South after Reconstruction.
  • Apply key vocabulary (segregation, boycott, civil rights) accurately in speaking and writing.
  • Evaluate solutions or claims about Civil Rights Movement using evidence.

Lesson outline (45 minutes)

  1. Warm-up & hook · 5 min
    • One woman refused to give up her bus seat. How did that spark a movement?
    • Think–pair–share: jot an answer, discuss with a partner, share out.
    • Record predictions/questions on the class chart.
  2. Mini-lesson (I do) · 11 min
    • Jim Crow laws enforced segregation in the South after Reconstruction.
    • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) ruled school segregation unconstitutional.
    • Nonviolent protest — the Montgomery Bus Boycott, sit-ins, the March on Washington — pressured change.
  3. Guided practice (We do) · 9 min
    • Model: What did Brown v. Board decide?
    • Together: Who was Rosa Parks?
    • Students attempt the next step on whiteboards before you reveal it.
  4. Independent practice (You do) · 11 min
    • 1. What did Brown v. Board decide?
    • 2. Who was Rosa Parks?
    • 3. What did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 do?
  5. Check for understanding · 5 min
    • Quick check: What did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 do?
    • Whiteboard response or digital poll — scan for misconceptions.
    • Pull a small group for reteach if needed.
  6. Closure & exit ticket · 4 min
    • Exit ticket: What did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 do?
    • Revisit the hook question — what do we know now?
    • Preview what's next.

Key ideas to teach

  • Jim Crow laws enforced segregation in the South after Reconstruction.
  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) ruled school segregation unconstitutional.
  • Nonviolent protest — the Montgomery Bus Boycott, sit-ins, the March on Washington — pressured change.
  • The Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) outlawed discrimination and protected voting.

Project idea

Driving question: How can young people create change in their community today? Plan a nonviolent campaign for a local issue.

Vocabulary

  • segregation: separating people by race
  • boycott: refusing to use a service as protest
  • civil rights: rights guaranteeing equal treatment
  • nonviolence: protest without physical force

Check for understanding

  1. What did Brown v. Board decide?
  2. Who was Rosa Parks?
  3. What did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 do?

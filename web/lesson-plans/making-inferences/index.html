Making inferences lesson plan

A ready-to-teach reading lesson for 3rd–7th grade. Every block is timed, from the hook to the exit ticket, and slides, a worksheet and an answer key are one click away.

Aligned to CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.RL.5.1 · PowerPoint, Google Slides, Canva, PDF, Word

Hook

A girl walks in dripping wet, holding a broken umbrella. What happened?

Learning objectives

  • Explain an inference is a conclusion based on text clues plus what you already know.
  • Apply key vocabulary (inference, evidence, schema) accurately in speaking and writing.
  • Compare solutions or claims about making inferences using evidence.

Lesson outline (45 minutes)

  1. Warm-up & hook · 5 min
    • A girl walks in dripping wet, holding a broken umbrella. What happened?
    • Think–pair–share: jot an answer, discuss with a partner, share out.
    • Record predictions/questions on the class chart.
  2. Mini-lesson (I do) · 11 min
    • An inference is a conclusion based on text clues plus what you already know.
    • Text clue + background knowledge = inference.
    • Good readers cite evidence to support their inferences.
  3. Guided practice (We do) · 9 min
    • Model: What two things do you combine to make an inference?
    • Together: 'Jake slammed the door and stomped upstairs.' How does Jake feel?
    • Students attempt the next step on whiteboards before you reveal it.
  4. Independent practice (You do) · 11 min
    • 1. What two things do you combine to make an inference?
    • 2. 'Jake slammed the door and stomped upstairs.' How does Jake feel?
    • 3. Why should you cite evidence for an inference?
  5. Check for understanding · 5 min
    • Quick check: Why should you cite evidence for an inference?
    • Whiteboard response or digital poll — scan for misconceptions.
    • Pull a small group for reteach if needed.
  6. Closure & exit ticket · 4 min
    • Exit ticket: Why should you cite evidence for an inference?
    • Revisit the hook question — what do we know now?
    • Preview what's next.

Key ideas to teach

  • An inference is a conclusion based on text clues plus what you already know.
  • Text clue + background knowledge = inference.
  • Good readers cite evidence to support their inferences.
  • Authors imply feelings and motives rather than stating them.

Project idea

Driving question: Create a mystery 'evidence box' for classmates to solve with inferences.

Vocabulary

  • inference: a conclusion reached from evidence and reasoning
  • evidence: details from the text
  • schema: background knowledge
  • imply: to suggest without stating directly

Check for understanding

  2. 'Jake slammed the door and stomped upstairs.' How does Jake feel?
  3. Why should you cite evidence for an inference?

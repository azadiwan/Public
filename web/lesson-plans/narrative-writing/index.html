Narrative writing lesson plan

A ready-to-teach english / ela lesson for 2nd–8th grade. Every block is timed, from the hook to the exit ticket, and slides, a worksheet and an answer key are one click away.

Aligned to CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.W.5.3 · PowerPoint, Google Slides, Canva, PDF, Word

Hook

Tell me about the scariest, funniest, or proudest moment of your summer — in 30 seconds.

Learning objectives

  • Explain a narrative has characters, a setting, a problem, rising action, a climax, and a resolution.
  • Apply key vocabulary (plot, climax, dialogue) accurately in speaking and writing.
  • Compare solutions or claims about narrative writing using evidence.

Lesson outline (45 minutes)

  1. Warm-up & hook · 5 min
    • Tell me about the scariest, funniest, or proudest moment of your summer — in 30 seconds.
    • Think–pair–share: jot an answer, discuss with a partner, share out.
    • Record predictions/questions on the class chart.
  2. Mini-lesson (I do) · 11 min
    • A narrative has characters, a setting, a problem, rising action, a climax, and a resolution.
    • Strong narratives show rather than tell, using sensory details and dialogue.
    • Transitions sequence events clearly.
  3. Guided practice (We do) · 9 min
    • Model: What is the climax of a story?
    • Together: Rewrite to 'show, not tell': 'I was nervous.'
    • Students attempt the next step on whiteboards before you reveal it.
  4. Independent practice (You do) · 11 min
    • 1. What is the climax of a story?
    • 2. Rewrite to 'show, not tell': 'I was nervous.'
    • 3. Name three transition words for sequence.
  5. Check for understanding · 5 min
    • Quick check: Name three transition words for sequence.
    • Whiteboard response or digital poll — scan for misconceptions.
    • Pull a small group for reteach if needed.
  6. Closure & exit ticket · 4 min
    • Exit ticket: Name three transition words for sequence.
    • Revisit the hook question — what do we know now?
    • Preview what's next.

Key ideas to teach

  • A narrative has characters, a setting, a problem, rising action, a climax, and a resolution.
  • Strong narratives show rather than tell, using sensory details and dialogue.
  • Transitions sequence events clearly.
  • A satisfying ending reflects on the experience or resolves the problem.

Project idea

Driving question: Publish a class anthology of personal narratives for families.

Vocabulary

  • plot: sequence of events
  • climax: the turning point
  • dialogue: characters' spoken words
  • sensory details: descriptions using the five senses

Check for understanding

  1. What is the climax of a story?
  2. Rewrite to 'show, not tell': 'I was nervous.'
  3. Name three transition words for sequence.
